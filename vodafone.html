02 The opportunity

Structure, collaboration and growth

In a complex organisation, good design depends on more than individual creativity. Teams need a shared understanding of the problem, a clear way to move from requirements to solutions, and the right environment to challenge assumptions and collaborate.

I saw an opportunity to bring more structure and consistency to the design process, make collaboration more intentional and help designers grow in their practice.

What I focused on

Process, workshops and mentorship.

01Creating a design process

I worked on establishing a clearer, more structured approach to design, helping teams move from initial requirements and problem definition through exploration, iteration and delivery.

The aim was to give designers and stakeholders a shared understanding of how to approach work, when to involve different people and how to make progress with greater clarity.

02Facilitating workshops

I planned and facilitated workshops across discovery, problem framing and ideation.

These sessions brought stakeholders together to explore challenges, challenge assumptions, exchange perspectives and align on possible solutions.

I used facilitation to turn conversations into tangible outcomes and help teams make decisions collaboratively.

03Mentoring designers

I supported designers through ongoing mentorship, design feedback and collaborative problem solving.

My focus was on helping them develop their thinking, build confidence in their decisions and become more independent in how they approached design challenges.

A design process the team could follow

From brief to handoff, with a clear step for every kind of conversation.

01Design brief

A one-pager from the product owner, reviewed together so design starts with full clarity on the problem, user and success criteria.

02Design

A focused design sprint, usually one week per flow.

03Designer and PO alignment

A mid-sprint check that design decisions reflect business goals and user benefits.

04Design review

A 30 minute walkthrough with the design lead on experience, flow logic and design system use.

05Iteration

Clear feedback and next steps, then refine.

06Async design review

Small changes reviewed within 24 hours, with no meeting needed.

07Technical feasibility

A quick sync with developers to catch blockers before handoff.

08Documentation and walkthrough

A tidy file with clear flows and specs, ready for handoff.

Three kinds of work, three levels of rigour

Not every request needs a full sprint, so the process scaled with the work.

01Quick amendments

Copy changes, image swaps and small content updates.

No design sprint. An async review checks design system use, usually delivered within one to two working days.

02Flow updates

Feature improvements and flow optimisations that affect screens.

A full sprint, usually one week per flow, with a sense-check with three people and a walkthrough in the weekly design review.

03Feature design

New features and larger revamps with multiple flows.

Broken into smaller flows, each a one-week sprint, with a kickoff with product to align on goals and success criteria.

A weekly rhythm

A steady cadence, with protected time to design.

DayWhat happens
SundayBrief delivered by the product owner, one-pager finalised, design kickoff begins.
MondayProduct review with product leadership. An optional sync between designer and product owner on solution direction.
Tuesday and WednesdayFocus days for design execution. No meetings unless urgent.
ThursdayOne-pager review for next week’s brief, design review for the current sprint and an async catch-up to close minor tickets.

What approval means

A shared checklist, so feedback is about the work and never personal.

Approved when

The design system is followed, or exceptions are justified.

The flow is smooth and user-friendly.

There is clear user logic or testing feedback.

The file is tidy and ready for developer handoff.

Sent back when

Basic design system rules are broken without reason.

Critical user paths are confusing or incomplete.

No user reasoning or testing is applied.

The file is disorganised or not ready to ship.

Rolling it out

A process only works if the team adopts it.

01Documentation

A central reference covering ticket types, touchpoints and the weekly rhythm, with real examples.

02Templates and tools

A one-pager template, ticket templates with clear statuses and message templates for async reviews.

03Kickoffs

One for the design team on process and expectations. One for product owners and the product team on briefs and how to work with design.

04Retrospectives

A quarterly retrospective on what is working, what is blocked and what to adjust.

Design System 2.0

A shared language for design and development.

  • Foundations. Typography, spacing, colour, elevation and iconography, defined once as variables so every screen stays connected to the system.
  • Components. Buttons, inputs, chips, tabs, cells, bottom sheets, navigation bars, forms and empty states, with their states and right to left Arabic versions.
  • Guides. A designer’s guide, a developer’s guide and naming conventions, so both sides use the same words for the same things.
  • Training. A hands-on session on variables and Figma housekeeping, with activities on colour and spacing.
Training  ·  Design system variables
NameVariables
What it means

Predefined tokens that represent colour, spacing, typography, effects and more. The building blocks of the design language.

Why it matters

Apply a variable instead of a value, and it stays connected to the system.

03 How I worked

Structure with participation

My approach combined structure with participation. I didn’t want process to become a set of rigid steps; I wanted it to help people do better work together.

That meant creating clarity without limiting exploration, making space for different perspectives and supporting designers throughout the process rather than only reviewing their final output.

05 What I took forward

What leading design means

This experience shaped my understanding of design leadership.

I learned that leading design isn’t simply about setting a direction or reviewing the work. It’s about creating the conditions for good work to happen: giving people structure, helping teams think together and developing designers who can make confident decisions independently.
